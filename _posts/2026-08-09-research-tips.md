### A Little Morale Boost
I want to say this upfront because I know - on a very visceral level - how absolutely, absurdly intimidating academic or clinical research papers can be. ***It is more than okay to take breaks, write extensive notes, or even abandon a topic for a little while.*** <u>***HOWEVER***</u>, curiosity is a gift and you deserve the accomplishment of teaching yourself something new and in-depth.

### A Research Sketchbook

Whenever I research things to teach myself, I <u>use a sketchbook</u>. More often than not, these are cheap ones that I find at stores like Five Below or at Walmart - you <b>don't</b> need a top-tier or extra pricey sketchbook to help you.

Having no lines means that I can write or draw whatever I need, wherever I need it. Sometimes I even add headings or page numbers or anything else I think is helpful (even my planner for day-to-day life is a sketchbook). This seems like a wild thing to do, but even if I have the capacity to read and re-read long (sometimes boring) notes - pictures are really helpful, too.

There's pretty solid research that visualizations and drawing help with information retention ([Rosen-O'Leary & Thompson, 2019 - PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1222159.pdf), [Balemans, et al. 2015](https://anatomypubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ase.1545), [Weiss, et al, 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Valerie-Weiss-2/publication/391431051_Enhancing_Medical_Education_Through_Drawing_A_Pathway_to_Deeper_Understanding_and_Retention/links/681a4a8dded433155742e56b/Enhancing-Medical-Education-Through-Drawing-A-Pathway-to-Deeper-Understanding-and-Retention.pdf)).

And I want you to remember a few things about using a sketchbook if you choose to do this:
 - You ***do not*** have to be an expert artist. Not in the slightest.
 - You ***can*** use stencils for shapes or rulers to help with lines - I have a ridiculously shakey hand, so I use rulers often.
 - You ***do not*** have to draw elaborately beautiful diagrams to explain something; it can be as simple as boxes with lines and/or arrows to connect ideas
 - If a funny, whimsical little cartoon or caricature helps you wrap your brain around something or remember it better, do it
 - A research sketchbook is **for you only**. You <u>can</u> share things to social media if you want, but it's often better to not worry about judgement or performing well.

### What Is Research In This Context
When I say 'research' in this specific context, I am referring to having a question about a topic or topics that you need to explain to yourself or learn more about. This is <u>not</u> 'research' in the sense that you will be exploring something new (alternatively, 'novel'). If you're familiar with the concept of a [review article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Review_article), that's what this type of research is. I personally focus on biomedical, biological, and biochemical topics, but that's my specific vein. Any of these tips can apply to other topics as well.

### How to Avoid Rabbit Holes
> Rabbit Hole = Scope Creep

There are two related tips I have for avoiding a never-ending series of rabbit holes or to bite off more than you can mentally chew at one time:

- Whenever you want to research a topic, come up with something that can be written out as a single question or sentence and ***WRITE IT DOWN***; it can be long, but should not be anymore than a few lines.
- The single question/sentence has to be ***as specific as possible***.

The idea with those tips to create a sort of conceptual 'swimlane' for yourself at the start.

> "Does vitamin D3 deficiency negatively impact mood?" is pretty open-ended, but if you refer back to your question as you find articles/research, you can answer the simple question "Does this help answer my question in any way?" Be very clear and concise (Yes/No answers only).

> A less specific question like "What does vitamin D3 do?" is going to take you on a wild ride (Trust me, I did this to myself once and lost an entire weekend of hyperfixated research. Doing laundry at 11 PM on a Sunday before work isn't fun.).

If it helps you organize, use a blank sheet of paper and write your question at the top of the page. If you have any questions or find topics/concepts you don't know, write them down under the question, but don't touch them just yet.

### Google Scholar (is Bae)
If you've never heard of it before, **[scholar.google.com](https://scholar.google.com/)** is an unsung hero in my life. It doesn't seem to aggregate everything in the world in terms of academic research, but it does provide a great starting place to look for actually helpful research. This won't be an absolute deep dive into the features and whatnot for Google Scholar itself, but it is VERY important that you **avoid biasing results when you put keywords in your search**.

Just an example of this, let's say we search for the word 'histamines':

### General Tips/Tricks

><b>LLMs and Agents</b>
- ***NEVER TRUST AN AI OR CHATBOT SUMMARY AT FACE-VALUE***. This makes me sound like the classic 'geriatric Millennial', but I promise that there is no good reason to trust and not verify.
- ***WHEN USING AN LLM OR AGENTS (especially if they can perform tasks or have access to the internet) INCLUDE AN INSTRUCTION THAT THEY MUST PROVIDE PLAIN-TEXT URL CITATIONS FOR INFORMATION***. The plain-text URL is a way, at least at first-glance, to protect yourself from clicking harmful links, but it also allows you to see if information was ad-libbed or summarized incorrectly (a form of 'hallucination').
- ***"IF CONFIDENCE IS LOW OR EVIDENCE IS UNAVAILABLE, EXPLICITLY STATE SO AND DO NOT PROVIDE AN ANSWER"*** you can include this as an instruction and it's a helpful risk mitigation, but not full-proof.

><b>Mitigating Biased Results in Searches</b>
- ***When searching for a topic, avoid assumptions*** - Assumptions in this instance, is the difference between the biased search of "Do antihistamines cause cognitive changes" and "Antihistamines and cognition"
- ***When searching for a topic, avoid negatives*** - Very similar to the previous tip, but "Poor cognition with antihistamines" is a negative assumption; a 'better' way to search would be to default back to 'antihistamines and cognition'
> Author's Note: Those examples were very niche, involving antihistamines and cognition; I chose those somewhat intentionally, as the answer is complex. First-generation antihistamines tend to have negative effects on cognition, whereas newer 'second-generation' antihistamines don't seem to have these negative effects ([Gary K, 2000](https://www.jacionline.org/article/S0091-6749(00)79554-6/pdf), [Shamil, et al,2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9810909/), [Jedrychowski, et al, 2013](https://ruj.uj.edu.pl/server/api/core/bitstreams/03044f36-0c9c-4a46-8ad9-a3250c869c4a/content)).



### Software and Tools
> I will always try to recommend open-source or free options with priority; paid products or those requiring subscriptions *may* be in this list, but I will try to denote them. **I have no affiliation with any of these platforms or companies, I just use them.**
- [Obsidian](https://obsidian.md/) has been something that is insanely helpful in my life. It is a note-taking application that is free (but does have paid tiers), but you can effectively create a 'graph' of your research topics and visualize things in multiple ways. I am a pretty 'vanilla' user of Obsidian, but there are some amazing tutorials and templates out there.
- [Zotero](https://www.zotero.org/) is an awesome tool, 'Your personal research assistant'.  You can install it, create an account for syncing across multiple devices, but the best thing is to add an extension to your browser of choice. It's pretty easy to organize and save papers, but you can also organize by topic and export citations.
